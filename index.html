As each cell grows, it keeps the concentration of each mRNA roughly constant. I watch single genes switch on and off in living cells to find out how.

Hi! I’m Bailey, a PhD candidate in Scott Berry’s group at the EMBL Australia Node in Single Molecule Science and the UNSW RNA Institute, UNSW Sydney. I study how cells control the transcription of individual genes to keep their mRNA levels in balance as they grow and divide, combining live-cell imaging, single-molecule counting and bespoke analysis pipelines.

Before UNSW, I studied Molecular Genetics and Biotechnology at Curtin University, then did honours with Ryu Takechi, testing whether Probucol could attenuate APOE4-associated neurodegeneration and helping establish the earliest brain changes after repeated sub-concussion. I expect to submit my thesis in February 2027.

Research

Cells keep their mRNA concentrations remarkably steady in the context of intrinsic and extrinsic noise. As a cell grows, transcript numbers rise in proportion to its size, and when production or decay is disturbed, cells rebalance the other to compensate. We know these adjustments happen, mostly from fixed-cell snapshots, but not how an individual gene tunes its activity, moment to moment, to make them happen.

My PhD asks how cells regulate transcription at the single-gene level to maintain mRNA homeostasis across the cell cycle and as cells change in size.

To answer it, I combine two kinds of measurement: live imaging shows when and how strongly a gene fires, and single-molecule counting shows how much mRNA that activity leaves behind. Using POLR2A as a test case, both show transcription scaling with cell size, and reveal which parameters of gene expression shift to keep mRNA concentration steady.

Seeing transcription live

Using CRISPR-Cas9 based gene editing and MS2-based reporters pioneered by Robert Singer, I insert a tag at a gene’s endogenous locus, so newly made RNA lights up the moment it is transcribed. I then utilise a lattice lightsheet microscope to follow these transcription sites in living cells over the course of hours, with high spatial and temporal resolution and little light damage, such that bursts of activity can be tracked through the cell cycle.

Counting every molecule

With single-molecule RNA-FISH, every mRNA shows up as a diffraction-limited spot that can be detected, assigned to a cell, and split between compartments. That turns an image into a measurement: how many transcripts each cell holds, the distribution of these mRNAs and how that number tracks with cell size.

From movies to quantitative measurements

reggie is the analysis package I’m building to turn hours of live-cell imaging into clean, comparable transcription traces. Nuclei are segmented with Cellpose-SAM and tracked with laptrack, and transcription sites are detected with ilastik. reggie then crops and recentres each cell, follows a single locus through time, measures its intensity against a matched control region and the nuclear background, and applies quality filters and photobleaching correction before anything is analysed.

Every threshold is set from quality-control plots rather than by eye, so researchers can readily adapt the pipeline to their own systems and biological questions.
