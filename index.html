As a cell grows, it keeps the concentration of each mRNA roughly constant. I watch single genes switch on and off in living cells to find out how.

No two cells in a population carry quite the same set of transcripts. Much of that variation comes from mRNA homeostasis: transcript numbers rise in proportion to cell size, so concentrations stay steady as cells grow. Cells achieve this by adjusting how quickly they make mRNA, and when either end of the RNA life cycle is disturbed, they rebalance synthesis and degradation to compensate, a process called mRNA buffering.

Most of what we know about these adjustments comes from modelling transcription in fixed cells, or from inducible systems that don’t behave like a gene in its natural context. How cells tune transcription of an individual gene, as it happens, remains largely unknown.

My PhD asks how cells regulate transcription at the single-gene level to maintain mRNA homeostasis across the cell cycle and as cells change in size.

To answer this, I use CRISPR-Cas9 to tag genes at their endogenous loci, so that transcription can be followed live with high spatial and temporal resolution on a lattice lightsheet microscope. I pair these movies with single-molecule RNA-FISH, which gives static snapshots of how many mRNAs of a given gene each cell contains. Using POLR2A as a test case, our live and fixed-cell data both show conserved cell-size scaling, with and without perturbations, and reveal how the parameters governing gene expression shift to keep mRNA concentration steady.

Together, these approaches aim to build a single framework for when and how transcription adapts inside living cells.

About

Hi! I’m Bailey. I completed my undergraduate degree in Molecular Genetics and Biotechnology at Curtin University in 2021, followed by honours in 2022 with Ryu Takechi at Curtin’s Health Innovation Research Institute.

For my honours project I investigated whether the drug Probucol could attenuate the neurodegenerative changes associated with the APOE4 genotype, using both in vivo and ex vivo analyses of transgenic mice expressing human APOE isoforms. I also worked on a rodent model of traumatic brain injury, where we established the earliest neuropathological changes that follow repetitive sub-concussion.

I’ve always held a keen interest in cell biology, and in 2023 I joined Scott Berry’s group at UNSW Sydney as a PhD candidate. My work focuses on visualising transcription and mRNA processing in living cells.

My interests span quantitative cell biology, live-cell imaging, transcription and gene expression, and cellular homeostasis.

Get in touch at b.hilesmurison@unsw.edu.au, or find my code on GitHub.
